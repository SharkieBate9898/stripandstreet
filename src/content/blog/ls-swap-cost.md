"Just LS swap it" is the most common advice in car culture, and for good reason. GM's LS V8s are compact, tough, everywhere and cheap to make power with. But the number people quote for an LS swap is almost always the price of the engine, and the engine is often the cheapest part of the job.

This guide breaks an LS swap into every line item that actually shows up on your credit card statement, then adds them up into three realistic budgets. All prices are as of November 2026 and come from current retailer listings and published cost guides. Where we're giving a range we built ourselves by adding those numbers up, we say so.

## The short answer

| Build | Who does the work | Rough total |
|---|---|---|
| Junkyard 5.3, factory computer, bolt-in kit | You | About $3,500–$9,000 (our estimate) |
| Mid-range: better engine, aftermarket ECU, more new parts | You | About $8,000–$15,000 (our estimate) |
| New crate LS3 with professional install | A shop | $20,000 and up |

The Olds Club's November 2026 cost guide lands in the same territory: $5,000 to $10,000 for an entry-level DIY build, $12,000 to $22,000 for a mid-range build, and $25,000 to $60,000+ for a full build with shop installation. The swing between those numbers comes down to three things: which engine, how much of the work you do yourself, and whether your car has a bolt-in kit or needs fabrication.

## Line item 1: The engine

The reason everybody swaps LS engines is that GM put them in millions of trucks and SUVs. The iron-block truck engines, especially the 5.3, are the budget favorite.

| Engine option | Typical price (as of Nov 2026) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Pull-it-yourself engine, self-serve yard | $379.99 + $54.99 core (complete fuel-injected engine with accessories) | Carolina Pick-n-Pull price list |
| Junkyard 5.3 | $200–$600 | U-Pull-It |
| Junkyard 5.3, bought pulled | $500–$1,500 | Bishop International |
| Junkyard 6.0 | $800–$2,000 | Bishop International |
| Remanufactured 5.3 long block | $2,000–$3,500 | Bishop International |
| New Chevrolet Performance LS3 long block (525 hp, part 19435110) | $8,108.60 | Crate Engine Depot |

A few things to know before you pick one:

- **Self-serve yards price by category, not by engine.** That's why a pull-it-yourself V8 can cost the same as a four-cylinder. You do the labor, and you need to bring the tools to get it out.
- **Get the whole donor package.** U-Pull-It recommends grabbing the computer, the full engine harness, the throttle body, the fuel rails and injectors, the accessory drive and the truck oil pan along with the engine. Those parts are worth far more bought new.
- **A "long block" isn't a running engine.** The LS3 long block above doesn't include the intake, injectors, accessories or computer. Chevrolet Performance sells complete crate engines and engine-and-control kits too, at higher prices.
- **Check it before you pay.** A compression test on a junkyard engine is cheap insurance.

## Line item 2: Wiring and engine management

This is where budgets start to slip. You have two main routes.

**Keep the factory computer.** You can rework the donor's harness yourself (cheapest, most time) or buy a standalone harness built for swaps. ICT Billet lists the [PSI Gen 3 LS standalone harness for drive-by-wire and a 4L60E](#affiliate) at $755 on sale. Either way, the factory computer has to be reprogrammed for your swap, including turning off the anti-theft (VATS) system, which PSI's listing notes is required. Bishop International puts a custom tune on the factory computer at $100 to $400.

**Go aftermarket.** The [Holley Terminator X for 24x LS engines with EV1 injectors (550-903)](#affiliate) is listed at $1,599.95 at two retailers. It includes the ECU, engine harness, injector harness, a Bosch wideband O2 sensor and a 3.5-inch touchscreen for tuning. Read the fine print: that version doesn't control the transmission or drive-by-wire throttle, so it suits a manual or a cable throttle, or a separate transmission controller.

Two details trip people up:

- **24x vs 58x.** Most Gen III engines use a 24x crank reluctor and most Gen IV engines use 58x, and harnesses and ECUs are sold for one or the other. PSI, for example, sells separate 24X and 58X harnesses. Know which engine you have before you order.
- **Drive-by-wire vs cable.** Match the harness and computer to the throttle body you're actually running.

Budget roughly $850 to $1,200 for a factory-computer setup with a new harness and tune, or $1,600 and up for a Terminator X (our estimates from the prices above).

## Line item 3: Mounts, crossmember and oil pan

Your car needs engine mounts, a transmission crossmember and an oil pan that clears its crossmember and steering. Truck oil pans are deep and often won't fit a car.

Holley's Swap Essentials kits are a useful price benchmark because they're sold in levels, and each level includes the one below it:

| Holley Swap Essentials level | What it adds | Starting price |
|---|---|---|
| Level 1 | Engine mounts, transmission mount and crossmember | $690–$780 |
| Level 2 | Oil pan for that chassis | $1,220–$1,370 |
| Level 3 | Hooker Blackheart stainless headers | $1,950–$2,300 |
| Level 4 | Header-back exhaust | $2,730–$3,500 |
| Level 5 | Accessory drive with A/C, power steering and alternator | $5,700–$5,900 |

Prices are from Holley's April 2026 overview and vary by car. Not every platform gets every level. If your car doesn't have a kit, you're into fabrication, and that's where DIY time and shop bills balloon.

## Line item 4: Exhaust

Bishop International lists long-tube headers at $300 to $800 and shorty or block-hugger headers at $150 to $400. Swap-specific headers that clear your car's chassis usually cost more than generic ones, which is why kits like Holley's bundle them in. After the headers you need a way to get exhaust to the back of the car, and if your state inspects emissions, catalytic converters too.

## Line item 5: Fuel system

An LS needs steady fuel pressure, often far more than the carbureted engine it may be replacing. If you're swapping into a car that already had fuel injection, you may be able to reuse or upgrade the existing pump. If not, you're buying a new pump, lines, a filter and a regulator.

The Olds Club guide puts a sump kit with an external pump at $300 to $600 and a complete EFI fuel system at $1,000 to $2,500. Don't cheap out here. A fuel system that can't keep up leans out the engine under load.

## Line item 6: Cooling

An aluminum radiator with electric fans runs $500 to $1,500 in the Olds Club guide. Some swaps reuse the factory radiator with adapters, but a V8 in a car that came with a four-cylinder usually needs more cooling. Steam lines, hoses and an overflow tank add more.

## Line item 7: Transmission and driveshaft

The cheapest route is to keep the donor's automatic. A self-serve yard in North Carolina lists an automatic transmission with converter at $219.99 plus a $24.99 core. Manual swaps cost more. The Olds Club guide lists a used T56 six-speed at $600 to $1,200, plus a bellhousing adapter and flywheel if you need them.

Almost every swap needs a new or shortened driveshaft. Budget $400 to $900 for a custom one. If you plan to make real power, your rear axle may need attention too.

## Line item 8: Labor

If you do it yourself, labor costs time instead of money. Plan on special tools, an engine hoist if you don't have one, and paying for welding or machine work you can't do.

If a shop does it, labor is usually the biggest line on the bill:

- **Bishop International:** add $2,000 to $5,000 or more for shop labor on a typical swap.
- **Olds Club (November 2026):** 60 to 120 hours at $150 to $200 an hour for a classic-car swap, or $12,000 to $25,000 in labor alone.

The difference comes down to the car. A platform with mature bolt-in kits takes far less time than one that needs mounts, a crossmember and an exhaust built from scratch.

### Mid-range build

Swap in a remanufactured engine or a stronger junkyard 6.0, a Terminator X, a full kit with headers, and a complete fuel system, and you're in the $8,000 to $15,000 range before any shop labor.

### Crate engine with a shop install

A new LS3 long block alone is about $8,100. Add the parts it doesn't include, a full fuel and cooling system, and shop labor, and $20,000 is a realistic starting point. The Olds Club guide puts full builds with shop installation at $25,000 to $60,000+.

## The costs nobody puts in the budget

- **Gaskets and seals.** A junkyard engine is the perfect time for a rear main seal, valve cover gaskets and a fresh water pump.
- **Fluids, fittings and hardware.** Individually cheap. Together, a few hundred dollars.
- **Tuning time.** Even a good base tune needs refinement once the car is running.
- **Brakes and suspension.** More power and a heavier engine can mean the old brakes and springs aren't enough anymore. Our [coilovers vs lowering springs guide](/coilovers-vs-lowering-springs/) covers the suspension side.
- **Emissions and registration.** Rules vary a lot by state. In places with strict emissions laws, a swapped car may need to meet the standards of the engine's model year with all its emissions equipment. Check your state's rules before you buy anything.

Our advice: whatever your parts list adds up to, set aside another 15 to 20 percent for the stuff you forgot.

## Is an LS swap worth it?

If your goal is reliable V8 power for less than the cost of a newer V8 car, yes, especially in a car with a mature swap scene and bolt-in kits. If your car has no kit support, price out the fabrication honestly first. Sometimes buying a car that already has a V8 is cheaper.
