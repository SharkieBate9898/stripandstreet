If you want a cheap, rear-drive, manual sports car in 2026, you basically have two choices from the factory: the **Subaru BRZ** (and its Toyota GR86 twin) or the **Mazda MX-5 Miata**. Both are light, both rev, both have huge aftermarkets, and both will make a twisty road the best part of your week.

They are not the same car, though. One is a tiny coupe with back seats and more power. The other is a two-seat roadster that weighs about 500 pounds less. Here's how they actually compare, new and used.

## The quick answer

- **Buy the Miata** if you want a top-down roadster, the lightest and most playful feel, and the lower price of entry. It's also the easier car to own as a pure weekend toy.
- **Buy the BRZ** if you want more torque, a fixed roof, a usable trunk and tiny rear seats for bags (or very small, very patient people). It's the better pick as a daily driver or a dedicated track car.
- **Either way,** buy a manual if you can. Both cars are at their best with three pedals.

## Power and performance

The BRZ wins on paper. Its 2.4-liter boxer makes 228 hp and 184 lb-ft, against 181 hp and 151 lb-ft from the Miata's 2.0. That extra torque is the thing you feel most. The BRZ pulls harder out of corners and on the highway without needing a downshift every time.

But the Miata is about 500 pounds lighter. Run the numbers and the gap shrinks: roughly 12.5 pounds per horsepower for the BRZ tS versus about 13.1 for the Miata Club. That's close enough that driver skill, tires and conditions will decide most real-world races, not the spec sheet.

Where they really differ is feel:

- **The Miata** is all about lightness. It rolls more, talks to you more, and feels fast at legal speeds. The 2.0 loves to rev to its 7,500 rpm redline, and with the top down it's an event even on a trip to the grocery store.
- **The BRZ** is flatter, more planted and more serious. It feels like a small track car that happens to have a roof. The tS adds Brembo brakes and STI-tuned Hitachi dampers, and every manual BRZ gets a Torsen limited-slip differential.

If you're planning to do [your first autocross](/first-autocross/), either car is a great tool. Both are common in local classes, and setup advice is easy to find for both.

## Practicality and daily driving

This is where the BRZ pulls ahead. It has a fixed roof, a pair of small rear seats and a folding rear seatback, plus 6.3 cubic feet of trunk. Fold the seat down and you can carry a set of spare wheels to the track, which is the classic BRZ party trick.

The Miata is a strict two-seater with about 5 cubic feet of trunk. That's enough for a weekend bag or a grocery run, and not much else. On the other hand, you get a roof that drops in seconds, and the RF gives you a power-folding hardtop if you want a quieter cabin. Buyer's guides note the soft top is loud on the highway, so try both if you can.

For fuel, Cars.com lists the 2026 Miata manual at 26 mpg city and 34 highway (29 combined). The BRZ is thirstier: when the 2.4 launched in 2022, the EPA rated it at 20/27/22 mpg with the manual and 21/30/25 with the automatic. The BRZ also wants premium fuel.

## Buying used: which generations to look at

Used is where both cars get really interesting, because the market has plenty of each.

### Subaru BRZ

- **First generation (2013–2020):** 2.0-liter boxer with 200 hp and 151 lb-ft. Manual cars from 2017 on got a bump to 205 hp and 156 lb-ft.
- **Second generation (2022 on):** the 2.4-liter, 228 hp and 184 lb-ft version you can buy new today. The extra 33 lb-ft of torque is the biggest upgrade, and it makes the newer car easier to drive quickly.

**What buyer's guides flag:** The 2013 model year has the most complaints of any BRZ year and was recalled for valve springs that could fail and damage the engine. The 2022 BRZ, the first year of the 2.4, drew reports of excess RTV sealant getting into the oil and clogging the oil pump pickup. SlashGear lists 2016, 2020 and 2024 among the best years to buy.

**Prices:** CarGurus market data from July 2026 put a three-year-old 2023 BRZ at about $28,100 on average and a 2025 at about $33,100.

### Mazda Miata (ND, 2016 on)

- **ND1 (2016–2018):** 155 hp and 148 lb-ft, with a 6,800 rpm redline.
- **ND2 (2019 on):** 181 hp and 151 lb-ft, with the redline raised to 7,500 rpm thanks to lighter pistons and rods, a stronger crank and bigger valves. It's the one to get if you can stretch.

**What buyer's guides flag:** Some 2016 and early 2017 manual cars had gear selection trouble under hard driving, and Mazda updated the gearbox early in 2017 production. ND1s also had a valve spring recall and some reports of worn second- and third-gear synchros. On any soft top, check the drains: clogged ones can soak the carpet and rust the floor. Clunks from the rear can be worn hub carrier bushings, which Mazda addressed in a service bulletin covering 2016 through 2021 cars.

**Prices:** CarGurus data from August 2026 had a 2021 Miata averaging about $26,700, a 2023 about $29,900 and a 2025 about $33,800. Used Miata prices climbed about 10% over the past year, so they hold value well.

If your budget is closer to $10,000, neither of these is the right car. Look at an older Miata instead. Our [NA vs NB Miata guide](/na-vs-nb-miata/) covers what to check.

## Modding and the aftermarket

Both cars have enormous aftermarkets, so you won't run out of parts for either.

- **The Miata** has probably the biggest small-car aftermarket in the world. Suspension, seats, roll bars, wheels and brakes are everywhere, and lots of parts are cheap. Start with our [Miata mods guide](/miata-mods-guide/) to see what's worth doing first.
- **The BRZ** shares nearly everything with the Toyota 86 and GR86, so you get parts from both brands' communities. Suspension is a popular first step. We used the BRZ/86 as the price example in [coilovers vs lowering springs](/coilovers-vs-lowering-springs/).

One thing to plan for: the BRZ's 18-inch tires are wider and pricier than the Miata's 17s, so consumables cost a bit more if you track the car.

## So which one should you buy?

**Get the Miata if:**
- You want a convertible, full stop
- You want the lowest price of entry, new or used
- It's a second car for weekends and back roads

**Get the BRZ if:**
- It has to work as a daily driver or carry a little more
- You want more torque and a fixed roof
- You plan to drive it to the track on its own tires and bring spares

**Either way:** drive both before you decide. They're closer on performance than the spec sheets suggest, and the right choice comes down to how you'll use the car. Pick the cleanest, best-documented example you can afford, and save some money for tires.
