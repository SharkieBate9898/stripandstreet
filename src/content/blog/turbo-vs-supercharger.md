Sooner or later every build hits the same wall: you've done the intake, the exhaust, the tune, and the car still isn't as fast as you want. That's when people start pricing forced induction, and the first question is always the same. **Turbo or supercharger?**

Both do the same basic job. They cram more air into the engine than it could pull in on its own, so it can burn more fuel and make more power. The difference is what drives them, and that one difference changes everything about how the car feels, how hard the install is, and what it costs.

Short answer: a **turbo** is the more efficient way to make big power and suits builds where you don't mind a little wait for boost. A **supercharger** gives you instant, linear response and is usually the simpler bolt-on, especially on V8s. Let's get into why.

## How a turbocharger works

A turbo is two fans on one shaft. On one side, a turbine wheel sits in the exhaust stream. Hot exhaust gas spins it on its way out of the engine. On the other side, a compressor wheel spins with it and pushes fresh air into the intake.

That's the big appeal: a turbo runs on energy that would otherwise go straight out the tailpipe. As J.D. Power puts it, "turbos create boost from exhaust gasses, whereas superchargers create boost from the rotation of the crankshaft."

The tradeoff is that a turbo needs exhaust flow to spin up. At low rpm, or when you stab the throttle from cruise, there's a delay before boost arrives. That's **turbo lag**, and the related idea, the rpm where the turbo starts making real boost, is called spool. A small turbo spools quickly but runs out of breath up top. A big turbo makes huge power but wakes up later.

To keep boost under control, turbo setups add a **wastegate** (which bypasses exhaust around the turbine once boost hits target) and usually a **blow-off or bypass valve** (which vents pressure when you lift off the throttle). Compressing air heats it, so most setups also run an **intercooler** to cool the charge before it reaches the engine.

For reference, J.D. Power says a turbocharger on a modern, unmodified car typically makes "anywhere from nine to 20 PSI" of boost.

## How a supercharger works

A supercharger is a compressor driven by a belt off the crankshaft, just like your alternator or water pump. Because it's mechanically tied to engine speed, it's making boost the moment the engine turns. No waiting for exhaust flow.

There are three main types you'll run into:

| Type | How it makes boost | How it feels | Typical look |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Roots** | Two lobed rotors move air into the intake | Instant boost and a broad torque curve, but runs hot | Big blower sticking up through the hood |
| **Twin-screw** | Two meshing screw rotors compress the air | Instant boost, cooler and more efficient than a Roots | Sits on top of the engine, often with a built-in intercooler |
| **Centrifugal** | An impeller spins at high speed, like the compressor half of a turbo | Boost climbs with rpm; soft down low, strong up top | Mounted at the front of the engine, looks like a turbo with a pulley |

SlashGear describes Roots blowers as delivering "instantaneous power with a broad torque curve, with no lag," with heat as their weak point. Twin-screws improve on that with "less leakage and power consumption." Centrifugal units are the most efficient of the three and "use less engine power to run and produce less heat," but because their boost rises with rpm, they don't hit hard until higher in the rev range.

The catch with every supercharger is **parasitic loss**. It takes engine power to spin the blower, and that drag is there all the time. At the extreme end, Jalopnik notes that the 14-71 blowers on Top Fuel dragsters need up to 1,000 hp just to turn. Your street car's blower won't eat anything close to that, but the principle is the same: some of the power it makes goes right back into driving it.

## Turbo vs supercharger: side by side

| | Turbocharger | Supercharger |
|---|---|---|
| **Driven by** | Exhaust gas | Belt off the crankshaft |
| **Throttle response** | Some lag, depends on turbo size | Instant (Roots/twin-screw), rpm-dependent (centrifugal) |
| **Power delivery** | Surges once it spools | Linear and predictable |
| **Efficiency** | Better; uses waste exhaust energy | Worse; costs crank power to drive |
| **Fuel economy off boost** | Little penalty when not in boost | Drag is always there |
| **Heat** | Lots, in the exhaust side and engine bay | Mostly in the intake charge |
| **Plumbing** | Exhaust manifold, oil (and sometimes coolant) lines, intercooler piping | Belt, pulley, brackets; often simpler |
| **Sound** | Whoosh and blow-off chatter | Whine (especially twin-screw and centrifugal) |
| **Power ceiling** | Very high with the right parts | High, but limited by pulley and belt |

Neither column wins outright. It comes down to what you want the car to do.

## How each one feels to drive

This is where people usually make up their minds.

A **supercharged** car feels like it has a bigger engine. Push the throttle and the response is right there, building smoothly to redline. J.D. Power describes superchargers as creating power "in a linear fashion, whereas turbochargers are less linear." That predictability is a big deal on a road course or autocross, where you want to steer the car with your right foot mid-corner.

A **turbo** car has more character, for better or worse. Off boost, it drives like the stock car. Once the turbo spools, it pulls hard. Modern turbos with good sizing spool fast enough that lag isn't a problem on the street, but you'll still notice the difference coming off a slow corner. On the other hand, nothing beats a well-matched turbo for big top-end numbers per dollar once you're past the basics.

Rule of thumb:

- **Daily driver, V8, or you want it to feel factory:** supercharger.
- **Small four-cylinder, chasing big numbers, or you like the rush:** turbo.
- **Track and autocross focus:** either works, but a positive-displacement supercharger or a quick-spooling turbo makes the car easier to drive at the limit.

## What a bolt-on kit actually costs

Kits vary wildly by car, so here are two real examples from popular platforms. Prices are as of November 2026 and don't include installation.

**Supercharger: 2018–2023 Ford Mustang GT.** ProCharger's centrifugal kits for the Coyote 5.0 start at **$7,249**, according to ProCharger. Its High Output system is rated for a **250+ horsepower gain** on 6.5 psi, and the Stage II system for **315+ horsepower** on 8.5 psi with a larger intercooler. ProCharger also cites a MotorTrend install on a stock 2018 GT that gained 276 rear-wheel horsepower on 93 octane. Complete systems include ECU tuning. CJ Pony Parts lists the intercooled High Output system from $7,999 and notes it isn't legal for sale or use in California.

**Turbo: 2006–2015 Mazda Miata (NC).** Flyin' Miata lists its Stage 1 turbo system from **$5,399**, and markets it as emissions legal for the NC. It showed as sold out when we checked, so availability may vary.

On top of the kit, budget for:

- **Installation** if you're not doing it yourself. A supercharger on a V8 is usually more bolt-on than a turbo kit, which involves exhaust work and oil lines.
- **Supporting parts.** Depending on power level, that can mean a bigger fuel pump, injectors, a stronger clutch, and better cooling.
- **Tuning and a dyno session** to make sure the air-fuel ratio and timing are safe.
- **Premium fuel.** Boosted engines generally need it to avoid knock.

## Don't forget the rest of the car

Doubling your engine's power without touching anything else is how cars end up on trailers. Before you add boost:

- **Check engine health.** Do a compression or leak-down test. Boost finds weak rings and head gaskets fast.
- **Upgrade the brakes.** More speed means more heat on every stop. Better pads and fresh high-temp fluid are the minimum.
- **Sort the suspension and tires.** If the car already feels floaty, more power won't fix it. Our [coilovers vs lowering springs guide](/coilovers-vs-lowering-springs/) is a good place to start.
- **Think about the clutch and drivetrain.** Stock clutches on many cars won't hold big torque gains for long.
- **Check your local emissions rules.** Many kits aren't legal for street use in California, and other states may inspect modified cars too. Look for a CARB EO number if it matters where you live.

## So which should you buy?

If you want a car that feels like it came from the factory with a bigger engine, with instant response, a predictable power curve and a cleaner install, get a **supercharger**. They're especially popular on V8 muscle cars, where bolt-on kits are well developed and the extra drag barely registers.

If you want the most power for your money, don't mind a little lag, and are comfortable with exhaust work and oil lines, get a **turbo**. It's the more efficient choice and the one with the higher ceiling, which is why it dominates on small-displacement cars like the Miata.

Whichever way you go, buy a complete kit from a reputable maker for your exact car, budget for the tune and supporting parts from day one, and fix the brakes before you need them. Boost is a lot more fun when the car can handle it.
